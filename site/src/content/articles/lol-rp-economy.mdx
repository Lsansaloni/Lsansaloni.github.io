You want a skin. It costs **1350 RP**. You open the store, buy the closest RP
bundle, and end up with **212 RP you will never spend on anything you
actually wanted**. Twenty-three days later, you're back in the store — because
that orphaned balance won't let you forget it.

Multiply that by a decade of players and a few hundred million purchases, and
you'll stop calling it an inconvenience. It's the product.

This is a designer's teardown of how League of Legends' premium currency
quietly re-prices everything it touches — not a rant about greed, and not a
conspiracy theory. Every pattern below is a deliberate, defensible design
decision. The interesting question isn't whether it's "evil". It's
**why it works so well**, and what a fairer version would look like.

## One currency is a decision. Two currencies are a business.

Strip away the fantasy names and the store sells exactly one thing: RP for
euros. But you never buy a skin for euros. You buy RP — and only then do you
"pay" for the skin.

That indirection does three jobs at once:

1. **It detaches the pain of paying from the act of buying.** Behavioral
   economics has a name for this: money loses its emotional charge the moment
   it's pre-paid and denominated in something else. Casino chips. Airline
   miles. RP. The wallet sting happens at the top-up, one blunt moment; the
   twenty skin purchases below it feel free-ish.
2. **It kills price memory.** "1350 RP" doesn't compute to euros in your head
   after the first week. Riot can re-tune bundle sizes, run regional
   promotions, or adjust the value of a euro-per-RP ratio across markets — and
   almost nobody recalculates their personal exchange rate.
3. **It makes the storefront, not the price tag, the unit of decision.** You
   stop comparing "is this skin worth €10?" and start asking "is this skin
   worth 1350 of my pile?" — a question with a very different answer.

None of this is hidden. It's standard premium-currency design, present in
every live-service game from Fortnite V-Bucks to Genshin's Genesis Crystals.
What makes League's implementation worth studying is how **tightly** the
bundle denominations are tuned against the skin price tiers.

## The gap is the product

Here's the mechanism in one sentence: **RP bundles are sized so that no
single bundle cleanly buys the skin you came for, and no combination of
bundles leaves you with zero leftover.**

{/* prettier-ignore */}
| What you want | What the store offers | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A 1350 RP skin | Bundles at awkward sizes around it | You either overshoot into leftover RP, or undershoot and top up again |
| "Just enough" RP | No exact-match tier exists | The remainder becomes a stored balance — your next trigger |
| To stop at one purchase | Leftover RP sits in the account | Returning to spend it *feels free* (sunk cost), so you return |

Two loops fall out of this, and they reinforce each other:

- **The top-up loop.** Leftover balance is almost-but-not-quite enough for
  the next tier up. The cheapest path to the thing you now want is another
  small purchase. The gap between "balance" and "price" is engineered to be
  frustratingly small — small enough to top up, large enough that you must.
- **The return loop.** Leftover RP is money you've already spent. Spending it
  later costs nothing *felt*. Every orphaned balance is a scheduled return
  visit with zero psychological cost of entry — the cheapest re-engagement
  mechanic Riot owns, and players opt in by buying anything at all.

Add rotating sales on a subset of the catalogue and you get a third beat: the
sale legitimizes waiting, which keeps the storefront relevant between
purchases, which keeps the currency top-of-mind. Urgency and patience, taking
turns.

## Anchoring: the tier ladder does the arguing

Skin prices aren't a flat rate — they're a ladder (roughly 575 / 875 / 1350
/ 1820 / 3250 RP across the classic tiers; verify current values before
publishing). The ladder isn't cosmetic. It's an anchoring machine:

- The **3250 tier exists** so 1820 feels reasonable. Nobody needs to sell you
  on 1350 — the absurd anchor two rungs up already did the work.
- **Chromas and lower tiers** absorb the price-sensitive segment without
  ever touching the mid-tier price. The floor isn't there to be bought; it's
  there to make the middle look like the deal.
- **Legacy/vault status** on select skins manufactures scarcity on items with
  zero marginal cost to release. The "will it come back?" uncertainty taxes
  patience and rewards immediate purchase — the same FOMO valve, repainted.

Every rung is priced against the rungs above and below it, not against the
marginal cost of a digital good (which is, famously, zero). The comparison
the player makes — "is this skin worth it?" — is answered entirely inside
Riot's own ladder. There is no external unit of account left to check
against. That's what the currency indirection bought.

## How I'd design it differently

If I were handed this economy, I wouldn't remove monetization — live-service
games need revenue, and cosmetics are the least pay-to-win shape available.
But three changes, none of which require a smaller revenue number:

1. **An exact-match purchase option.** Let players buy any skin directly for
   its euro-equivalent price, beside the RP path. Transparency is a
   competitive feature: Warframe lets you trade platinum with other players
   and its store survives it. If the skin's value holds up against a direct
   price tag, the direct tag becomes free marketing for the RP path anyway.
2. **A visible "complete your balance" micro-top-up.** If the leftover is the
   mechanic, own it: when a player is 200 RP short, offer the exact 200 RP
   top-up, one click, priced honestly. The loop stays; the frustration — the
   part that breeds forum resentment and refund pressure — goes.
3. **One-page price transparency.** €-per-RP by bundle size, published and
   current. Bundles that reward scale should be *legible* rewards, not
   puzzles. Players don't rage against premium pricing; they rage against
   being unable to do the arithmetic.

The pattern underneath all three: **keep the economy's power, drop its
opacity.** Premium currency survives contact with transparency. Leftover RP
*as engineered friction* does not — and a design that can't survive being
understood probably deserves not to.

## What's next in this series

League is the extraction case study. The other two shapes of live-service
economy deserve equal treatment:

- **The WoW Token** — what happens when a publisher *legalizes* real-money
  trading and builds an official exchange around the black market it just
  made redundant.
- **Warframe's platinum** — an emergent player-to-player market the
  developers almost stumbled into, and why it's the most humane economy in
  the genre.

Same method each time: real numbers, real mechanisms, and a redesign I'd
defend in a room full of producers.
